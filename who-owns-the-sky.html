Research Project

Who Owns
the Sky?

Islamic Jurisprudence and the New Tragedy of Celestial Common

Research conducted byEmiola Habeeb

Research Fellow, AQIRI

Research begins 15 November 2026

Research Abstract

Abstract

In 1968 Garrett Hardin described the Tragedy of the Commons: shared resources that no one regulates are exhausted by individuals pursuing their own interest. Elinor Ostrom later showed that communities can govern such resources through collective rules. Neither of them foresaw the commons that now presses hardest on humanity, the sky above us.

In barely a decade, outer space has moved from state monopoly to commercial frontier. Mega-constellations crowd low-Earth orbit, and the risk of a Kessler cascade is no longer theoretical. At the same time, weather modification and proposals for solar geoengineering threaten to move rainfall and sunlight across borders. The existing legal framework (the Outer Space Treaty, the ENMOD Convention and ITU orbital regulation) was designed for states. It offers no enforceable answer to private exploitation or to the question Jeff Goodell asks: “Whose hand will be on the thermostat?”

This project asks what Islamic jurisprudence, a mature legal tradition that environmental and space governance has largely ignored, can contribute. It makes four interlocking arguments:

  1. Istikhlāf (vicegerency) treats humanity as a trustee of creation, not its owner. This reverses the starting point of possession-based property theory.
  2. Ḥimā (the protected commons) offers a community-governed model for orbital bands and the stratosphere.
  3. Lā ḍarar wa lā ḍirār (“no harm and no reciprocal harm”) supplies a principle of liability for harm that crosses borders, whoever causes it.
  4. Maṣlaḥa mursala (public-interest reasoning) provides the method for extending the law responsibly into domains the classical jurists could not have imagined.

It will bring Islamic legal doctrine into conversation with commons theory, space law and the debate on geoengineering governance. The research aims to add a missing voice to one of the defining governance challenges of this century.
